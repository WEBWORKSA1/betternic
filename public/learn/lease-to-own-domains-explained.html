Lease-to-Own Domains Explained: How Domain Payment Plans Work

How domain lease-to-own and payment plans work for buyers and sellers: terms, markups, ownership, defaults and how to set them up safely.

Lease-to-own (LTO) lets a buyer start using a premium domain immediately and pay for it in monthly installments, with ownership transferring after the final payment. It has become one of the most effective ways to close aftermarket deals, because many startups can afford $500 a month long before they can afford $20,000 up front.

How it works

  1. Seller and buyer agree a price, term and any down payment.
  2. The buyer receives the right to use the domain — usually by controlling DNS — while the seller or a custody provider keeps legal ownership.
  3. The buyer pays monthly. The seller (or platform) receives the payments.
  4. After the final payment, the domain is transferred to the buyer.
  5. If the buyer stops paying, the agreement typically ends, the seller keeps the payments received and the domain returns to the seller.

Why sellers offer it

  • More buyers can say yes.
  • Higher total price thanks to the financing markup.
  • Recurring income that can cover portfolio renewals.

Why buyers choose it

  • Get the right name now, pay as the business grows.
  • No need to borrow from a bank.
  • Early payoff is usually allowed.

Protecting both sides

  • Use a written agreement covering term, payments, use rights, default and early payoff.
  • Keep the domain at a registrar account the buyer cannot transfer from until paid.
  • Ask for a down payment on long terms.
  • Buyers should confirm the seller actually owns the domain before paying anything.
