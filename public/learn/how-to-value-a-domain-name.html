How to Value a Domain Name: The Investor's Framework

A practical framework for valuing domain names: length, words, extension, liquidity, comparable sales, traffic and buyer demand — with worked examples.

Domain valuation is part science, part judgement. Two people can look at the same name and be thousands of dollars apart, and both can be reasonable, because a domain's price depends on who is buying and why. This guide gives you a repeatable framework that professional investors use, so your numbers are defensible whether you are buying, selling or deciding what to renew.

1. Separate wholesale value from retail value

Every serious valuation starts with two numbers. Wholesale value is what another investor would pay you today for quick liquidity. Retail value is what an end user — a business that needs that exact name — might pay. Retail is often five to twenty times wholesale. The gap is the reward for patience, marketing and finding the right buyer. Confusing the two is the most common valuation mistake: an investor offers wholesale, a seller expects retail, and the deal dies.

2. Score the name itself

Start with the intrinsic qualities buyers consistently pay for:

  • Length. Shorter is scarcer. Every three-letter .com is registered, which is why they trade with a market floor.
  • Real words. Common dictionary words in .com are finite and widely understood. Frequency matters: "bright" beats "brisk".
  • Commercial meaning. Words tied to money-making categories — insurance, loans, health, travel, software — attract buyers with budgets.
  • Pronounceability and spelling. Pass the "radio test": if someone hears it once, can they type it correctly?
  • No hyphens or awkward numbers. Each hyphen or stray digit removes buyers.

3. Weight the extension

.com remains the default for businesses worldwide and has the deepest resale market. Other extensions can be excellent for a specific buyer — .ai for AI startups, .io for developer tools, a country code for a local business — but their resale pools are smaller. A practical rule: value the name as a .com, then apply a discount that reflects how many buyers actually shop in that extension.

4. Check liquidity

Liquidity is how quickly you could sell at a fair price. Short patterns (LLL.com, NNNN.com), top dictionary words and strong two-word .com names are liquid: there are always investors ready to buy at some price. Long phrases and invented names are illiquid — they may be worth a lot to one buyer, but finding that buyer can take years. Lenders, by the way, care almost entirely about liquidity, which is why domain-backed financing uses wholesale values.

5. Find comparable sales

Comparable sales anchor a valuation in reality. Look for reported sales of names with the same structure: same length, same extension, similar keyword strength. Databases such as NameBio and reports such as DNJournal's sales charts are the usual sources. Be careful: reported sales skew toward successful, publicised deals, and private brokered sales are under-reported. Use comps to set a range, not a single number.

6. Add traffic, history and revenue

Type-in traffic, backlinks and existing revenue can add value beyond the name. Check the website history to see whether a domain hosted a real business, and screen its reputation — spam history can subtract value. If a domain earns money, value the income separately (a common shortcut is a multiple of annual net profit) and add it to the name's value.

7. Subtract legal risk

A domain containing someone else's trademark is worth far less, because a UDRP complaint could take it away and few legitimate buyers will touch it. Run a trademark check before you assign any value.

8. Put it together

Write down a wholesale floor, a realistic retail range and a stretch number for the perfect buyer. Price your listing near the lower end of the retail range if you want inquiries, use the asking price calculator to set a minimum offer, and offer lease-to-own to widen the buyer pool.

Common mistakes

  • Using an automated number as the final price.
  • Valuing based on what you paid or what you hope for.
  • Ignoring renewal costs when holding illiquid names for years.
  • Pricing every name in a portfolio the same way.
